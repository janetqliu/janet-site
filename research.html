Research experience

My research asks a practical question: when we put technology in front of learners, who actually benefits? Educational tools are often judged by their average effect, but an average can hide a lot. A tutor that helps strong students pull further ahead while struggling students stay put can look like a success on paper. This pattern, often called the Matthew effect, is the thread running through most of my current work.

I came to this question through language. Growing up between Mandarin and English, and later working on language acquisition and Spanish linguistics research, I saw how much a learner's starting point shapes what they get out of an experience. At Carnegie Mellon, I carried that interest into math education, studying how students learn with an AI-based tutor compared with paper practice.

Methodologically, I work mostly with classroom log data and pre- and post-test scores. I use mixed-effects models in R to account for students being nested within classes and schools, and learning-curve methods such as iAFM to estimate how quickly students improve with practice. I care about doing this carefully: preregistering hypotheses, separating confirmatory from exploratory results, and reporting what didn't work alongside what did.

Long term, I want to do research in industry, close to the products students actually use, so that findings turn into better tools rather than staying in papers.
